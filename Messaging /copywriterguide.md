Content building blocks
The formula for best-in-class editorial content is messaging + writer = copy. Let’s break it all down.

Messaging
Strong, clear, and accurate messaging is the foundation of good copy. That’s why we’ve created messaging frameworks–structured, internal documents that clarify what we offer customers and define our approach to constructing consistent and effective marketing materials. One messaging framework can inform an entire campaign, supporting repeatable excellence in our speed-to-execution model.

Writers
When writers receive complete input detailing what we’re saying, why we’re saying it, who the audience is, and what our business objectives are, they have all the tools they need to craft effective copy.

Writers do not change the intent of the content. They massage the delivery to build an engaging experience for our customers.

Reserve writing budget for customer-facing assets, especially if you’re creating something brand new. Writers are here to take the work of creating copy off your plate–so you can focus on providing a rich messaging framework.

The Approachable Expert is deeply knowledgeable in their domain and conveys complex ideas with ease and warmth. Their expertise is both credible and relatable. They genuinely find the best in everyone–making their audience feel understood, valued, and supported.

What it is:
Brand personality is the human-like set of qualities that give SAP its unique voice in the marketplace. This encompasses traits such as friendliness, reliability, innovation, and sophistication.

What it’s for:
Its purpose is to establish a deeper emotional connection with our audience. It guides how the brand communicates across various platforms, influencing everything from the tone of copywriting to the style of visuals.

How we write
Bringing out your best writing at SAP means telling a compelling story in the style of an approachable expert. The pillars below tell you how to get there.

Be brief. Be bright. Be gone.
Our overarching goal is to simplify the way we communicate in our writing. We want to take a complex idea or situation and explain it clearly. Details and nuance still matter, but in a way that makes practical sense. This approach makes content easier to consume, supports the mobile experience, and prevents the copy from running too long when translated into non-English languages.

Before
“Customer preferences can turn on a dime. New opportunities–and threats–can appear out of the blue. Only with fully automated processes and end-to-end ERP software can you grasp fleeting opportunities to run with efficiency, get all your enterprise working as one at global scale, and comply with regulations.”

After
"Customer preferences can change overnight. New opportunities—and threats—might appear at any time. Fully automated processes and end-to-end ERP software help align your people to work toward the same goal.”

Before
“Technical limitations and landscape complexity hinder a company’s ability to digitalize end-to-end business processes and explore new business opportunities and models. To combat these challenges, organizations need a solution that supports integration of legacy enterprise systems and new cloud-based applications.”

After
“The complexity and technical limitations of a legacy system can hold your business back. A solution that lets you integrate legacy enterprise systems with new cloud-based applications can help you face these challenges head on.”

What this looks like:
Brief: Delete flowery, extraneous language.
Bright: Swiftly and honestly address problems, but make a solution and positive outlook readily available.
Gone: Communicate a central idea in the simplest way possible, guiding the customer to the next phase of the journey.
Move to scenario-based copy
Stories make for the most memorable conversations. Keep it short, but make sure there’s a beginning, middle, and end to your scenario. You can use quick, pithy transitions and introductory phrases instead of going straight to commands. Better connect with your audience through active instead of passive voice.

Before
“Learn how to become a customer-centric business to raise conversion rates, brand loyalty, and revenue with customer experience (CX) and CRM solutions.”

After
“When you make customers top priority, your bottom line grows. Happy customers become loyal ones–thanks to a consistent customer experience that helps raise conversion rates.”

Before
“See how service-driven firms can elevate, accelerate, and differentiate with SAP S/4HANA Cloud at the heart of their business.”

After
“When your services stand out, customers take notice. Create an elevated customer experience with tools that set your business apart.”

What this looks like:
Create a story hook, however big or small.
Address customer pain points and opportunities. Don’t gloss over problems and immediately tout our solutions.
Showcase clear benefits and positive outcomes.
Moderate use of commands (imperative phrases)
In our effort to be clear and concise, we sometimes miss out on being approachable by using too many imperative phrases in a row. Imperatives still work well in bullets and headlines, but they shouldn’t be used consecutively–especially in body copy. It sounds like we’re commanding our readers instead of offering a conversation.

Before
Shorten time to market with API-first, headless architecture. Reach the right consumers at the right moment and on the right channel. Embed analytics for insight-led decisions that enhance customer lifetime value (CLV).”

After
“An exceptional customer experience starts by putting your audience at the forefront of everything you do. Unify customer data across your enterprise and use AI to build unique customer profiles that can deepen your understanding.”

Before
“Reduce your costs and your risk by connecting with a community of global suppliers. Discover our fully digital solution today and transform processes with SAP Business Network Commerce Automation.”

After
“Process transformation doesn’t have to come with high costs and risk. Connecting with a community of global suppliers can help you automate your commerce processes with confidence.”

What this looks like:
Sentences in body copy don’t start with consecutive imperatives.
Titles, headlines, body copy, and CTAs do not all begin with imperatives.
Copy invites the reader to take one targeted action rather than reading like a series of commands.
Incorporate contractions
Contractions help evoke a natural speaking cadence. Address the reader directly in the second person, using contractions like “you’re.” All contractions are permitted–they save valuable space.

Before
“Break down operational and planning silos and improve teamwork and efficiency in the planning process.”

After
“There’s no way around it—to improve efficiency, performance, and resilience, you’ll need to align people and processes across the company.”

Before
“Vendor lock-in forces organizations to continue with the same technology, even if it is getting older, is not compatible with modern technologies, or is not flexible enough to integrate with others.”

After
“Vendor lock-in keeps you stuck with the same technology—even if it’s older, incompatible with new tech, or isn’t flexible enough to integrate with other applications.”

Avoid clichés
Copy can be clear, informal, and catchy without using colloquialisms or platitudes. We want to sound human while also sounding professional–and keep our copy easy to localize.

Before
“SAP Cloud ERP and SAP Business Technology Platform go together like peanut butter and jelly.”

After
“SAP Cloud ERP and SAP Business Technology Platform go together like two puzzle pieces.”

Before
“By optimizing and automating fragmented processes, you can provide a harmonized experience.”

After
“A better user experience starts with streamlining scattered business processes.”

What this looks like:
Create copy that easily translates, for streamlined localization.
Use plain, direct language in place of corporate jargon.
Demystify concepts instead of overcomplicating them.
Watch out for "word salad"
Sometimes, we use buzz phrases or create a collection of words that are “correct” but say nothing specific or useful. It often happens when we’re trying to cram too much info into one sentence. Once certain phrases become trendy, they often lose meaning and context. We need to turn these phrases into copy that addresses real customer pain points and advances the journey.

Before
“Benefit from a holistic, human-centered approach to innovation that addresses all areas of your business while balancing the needs of people with processes and technology to deliver uniquely tailored, high-value solutions that provide business value at scale.”

After
“Innovation means nothing if your people get lost in technology and processes. Keep them at the center of your business with high-value solutions to help you innovate at scale.”

Before
“When adopted strategically, integration can bridge silos across applications, data, and processes, helping facilitate enterprise agility and business model flexibility.”

After
“Bring speed and flexibility to your business by unifying siloed applications, data, and processes.”

What this looks like:
Make copy visually appealing and digestible with subheads, bolding, bullets, etc.
Ensure copy flows seamlessly when spoken out loud.
Untangle complex phrases and ideas into distinct, separate thoughts that the reader can follow.
Avoid gender-specific terms
Avoid gender-specific terms; use an appropriately neutral singular noun, such as “user” or “administrator,” or use a plural.

Replace “he” or “she” or “he/she” with “they.”

Referencing SAP
Use the first-person plural to reference SAP (“we/us”) whenever possible. The next best option is to refer to SAP as SAP. Use “SAP SE” or another SAP legal entity name (SAP America, for example) only where it is necessary to distinguish it from SAP SE or the SAP Group.
